CWC Health is a mobile health navigation prototype co-designed with Community
Wellness Center members, peer support specialists, and a Rutgers team spanning
behavioral health and engineering. The current study build has four tabs
(Nearby, My Health, Learn, More) and a persistent Help Now button. It finds
nearby pharmacies and clinics from OpenStreetMap, shows source-labeled health
education, and keeps crisis lines one tap away. It has no user accounts, no
third-party analytics, and no device location tracking.

\section{Introduction}
% Framing sources: Box Final Proposal narrative; notes/research-framing.md; Related Work cites.
% Do not insert focus-group N, quotes, or survey findings here until Method/Results are sourced.
Finding health information and nearby care now happens on a phone. Appointments,
pharmacy locations, health articles, and crisis numbers live in separate portals,
search results, and social feeds, and their quality is hard to
judge~\cite{Jia2021OHIS,DiazHernandez2024HealthInfo,Zhao2022OlderOHIS,Pedroso2026SocialMedia}.
For people with mental health, substance use, or co-occurring physical conditions,
and especially those living in poverty or without stable housing, the problem is
worse: lower digital literacy, fragmented services, and a reasonable distrust of
apps that collect a lot of
data~\cite{Whitehead2023Barriers,Ding2025mHealth,Dwyer2025TechUse,Fitzpatrick2025DigitalEquity,Epp2025CoLeadership}.
Community Wellness Centers (CWCs) already offer low-barrier, peer-delivered
support. Their members still need a simple way to find local health resources
and keep track of their own health information without signing up for an app
that tracks them.

Most digital health tools assume accounts, continuous tracking, clinical decision
support, or a chatbot. We ask a different question:
\emph{How much useful, contextually personalized health support can a mobile
application provide while collecting and retaining as little sensitive user data
as possible?} ``Personalized'' here means access shaped by the user's context
(where they are, what health information they keep on the phone). It does not
mean diagnosis or treatment recommendations. We work on this question through
equity-centered co-design with CWC members, peer support specialists, a community
advisory board, and a Rutgers team from behavioral health and electrical and
computer engineering. The prototype, CWC~Health, is a Flutter app with four tabs
(Nearby, My~Health, Learn, More) and a persistent Help~Now button. The current
study build focuses on navigation that is easy to find, source-labeled health
articles, crisis dialers that stay off unless a build enables them, and a live
Nearby tab that geocodes a town name and queries OpenStreetMap instead of using
device GPS. It has no account system and no third-party analytics.

This paper describes the system design of the study prototype and relates it to
prior work on online health information seeking, mHealth adoption barriers, and
participatory digital health. The method and results sections will be filled in
from study materials as they are finalized. We do not claim clinical efficacy.

\subsection{Online Health Information Seeking and Fragmented Access}
People look for health information online, but it is spread across websites,
portals, and social platforms and is hard to
evaluate~\cite{Jia2021OHIS,DiazHernandez2024HealthInfo,Zhao2022OlderOHIS}.
A US national survey (HINTS 2024) found that most social media users engage with
health content there, and most of them also believe much of it is false or
misleading~\cite{Pedroso2026SocialMedia}. Policy commentary ties digital access
programs directly to digital health equity for low-income
populations~\cite{Fitzpatrick2025DigitalEquity}. ``Social prescribing,'' where a
clinician or community worker refers someone to non-medical local supports,
depends on the same thing: a reliable map of what is nearby~\cite{Lancet2025SocialPrescribing}.
This is the gap CWC~Health targets: one place to find nearby resources and
trusted education, without expecting users to assemble a care pathway on their own.

\subsection{Barriers to Mobile and Digital Health Engagement}
A qualitative systematic review of 34 studies found the same barriers to digital
health across culturally and linguistically diverse groups: access, literacy,
language, trust, and usability~\cite{Whitehead2023Barriers}. In an integrated
youth-services network, users and staff said mHealth lowered barriers to
in-person care but raised privacy, design, and sustainability concerns, and they
asked for co-design~\cite{Ding2025mHealth}. Digital literacy is now treated as a
social determinant of health, with people with serious mental illness at the
highest risk of being left out~\cite{Dwyer2025TechUse}. These are the reasons
CWC~Health uses a flat four-tab layout, labels the source on every Learn article,
and ships without accounts or analytics.

\subsection{Co-Design and Participatory Digital Health}
Community-based participatory research and co-production hold that community
partners should share decision-making, and that this produces more relevant,
more trusted work~\cite{Opara2025CBPR,PapastavrouBrooks2023Principles}.
Epp et al.\ make the same argument for lived-experience co-leadership in mental
and substance-use care~\cite{Epp2025CoLeadership}. Veldmeijer and van Os warn
that the ``participatory'' label is often absorbed by institutions without any
real transfer of power~\cite{Veldmeijer2025Participatory}. Two recent app
projects show what co-design looks like in practice: adapting App4Independence
for opioid use disorder with 6 lived experts and 8 clinicians, and formative
interviews and focus groups with a community advisory board for a
wellness-enhanced contingency-management app~\cite{Darcey2024A4iO,Deshais2026WECM}.
CWC~Health follows the same approach but for health navigation: nearby care,
personal health organization, education, and urgent support, with as little
stored data as possible and no clinical decision support or generative AI.

\subsection{Design Goals}
CWC Health is a health-navigation app, not a clinical decision-support system.
It helps a user find care, keep their own health information on their phone,
read health articles with a named source, and reach urgent help fast. It does
not diagnose or recommend treatment. Three goals drive the design: (i)~collect
as little data as possible (no accounts, no third-party analytics), (ii)~a
layout that CWC members can find their way around in co-design sessions, and
(iii)~live resource discovery that does not depend on tracking the user's
location. The Flutter study prototype implements the navigation shell and the
live Nearby tab. Several My~Health and offline-directory behaviors are still
design targets in the project specification; each is marked as such below.

\subsection{Application Structure}
The study build is a Flutter app with four bottom tabs (Nearby, My~Health,
Learn, More) and a Help~Now button in the header, so urgent actions are one tap
away from any screen. Nearby is the default tab. Two compile-time flags gate
network behavior. \texttt{LIVE\_NEARBY} (default off) swaps the static Nearby
demo list for live OpenStreetMap results. \texttt{HELP\_NOW\_LIVE} (default off)
turns the 988, 911, and Poison Control buttons into real \texttt{tel:} and
\texttt{sms:} launches. In the current build, My~Health and Learn show sample
content for co-design feedback, and More opens placeholder settings and support
pages. Its Erase button currently explains that nothing is stored on the phone
yet.

\subsection{Privacy and Data Minimization}
The prototype has no user accounts, stores no login credentials, and includes no
third-party analytics SDKs (its only runtime dependencies are \texttt{http},
\texttt{url\_launcher}, and \texttt{shared\_preferences}). Live Nearby keeps
two things on the device: the last successful listing and the town geocode
point. There is no identity and no GPS history. Help~Now dialers stay off
unless a build sets \texttt{HELP\_NOW\_LIVE}, so a demo cannot place a real
crisis call by accident. The feature specification also calls for an optional
PIN on My~Health, encrypted on-device records, and a working one-tap erase.
None of those are in the current study build, and we do not claim them here.

\subsection{Location-Aware Nearby Services}
With \texttt{LIVE\_NEARBY} on, Nearby geocodes a fixed town name (New Brunswick,
NJ, in the v1 engineering demo) through Nominatim and queries OpenStreetMap
Overpass for pharmacies, clinics, and urgent care nearby. It tries three
Overpass mirrors in order, because Overpass rate-limits per IP and a room of
phones on one Wi-Fi network shares an IP. An optional Google Places lookup
falls through to OpenStreetMap if no billed key is set. The live path never
requests location permission and uses no background location APIs. Results are
deduplicated, time-stamped, and cached. If every source fails, the tab shows an
error and a retry button. It never falls back to the demo list, because a user
cannot tell a fake pharmacy from a real one. Directions open OpenStreetMap on
web or a native \texttt{geo:} link on device, with no embedded map SDK. Builds
with the flag off keep the static New~Brunswick demo list for meetings and tests.

\subsection{On-Device Personal Health Information}
My~Health shows appointments, medications, providers, and a wallet card. In the
current build these are sample constants, not saved user data, and the screen
says My~Health is not PIN-locked yet. Learn has six topics with short articles
and a named source on each (for example, public-health agencies). It does not
generate AI answers. The longer-term target is a local-only personal-health
store with an optional PIN and a working erase.
