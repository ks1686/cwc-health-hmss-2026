\begin{abstract}
CWC Health is a privacy-preserving mobile health navigation prototype co-designed
with Community Wellness Center (CWC) members, peer support specialists, and a
Rutgers team in behavioral health and engineering. Stage~1 needs assessment used
five focus groups (2 virtual peer-support sessions; 3 in-person member sessions
at New Jersey CWCs; compensated attendance $n{=}7$ peer support specialists and
$n{=}38$ members) plus Qualtrics pre-surveys (member demographics $n{=}41$;
peer-support items $n{\approx}9$--10). Participants asked for plain-language tools,
trusted local resources, peer-supported onboarding, and clear privacy limits.
The current study build is a Flutter app with four tabs (Nearby, My~Health, Learn,
More) and a Help~Now control~\cite{FlutterDocs2026}. Live Nearby geocodes a town
name and queries OpenStreetMap (no device GPS). The app has no user accounts and
no third-party analytics. This paper reports Stage~1 methods and findings, the
as-built study prototype, and how community themes shaped design choices. We do
not claim clinical efficacy or Stage~3 usability results yet.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
mobile health, personalized health, health navigation, privacy by design,

\section{Introduction}
% Framing: notes/research-framing.md; Related Work cites; team proposal narrative (outside git).
% Do not insert focus-group N, quotes, or survey findings here until Method/Results are sourced.
Finding health information and nearby care now happens on a phone. Appointments,
pharmacy locations, health articles, and crisis numbers live in separate portals,
search results, and social feeds, and their quality is hard to
judge~\cite{Jia2021OHIS,DiazHernandez2024HealthInfo,Zhao2022OlderOHIS,Pedroso2026SocialMedia}.
For people with mental health, substance use, or co-occurring physical conditions,
and especially those living in poverty or without stable housing, the problem is
worse: lower digital literacy, fragmented services, and a reasonable distrust of
apps that collect a lot of
data~\cite{Whitehead2023Barriers,Ding2025mHealth,Dwyer2025TechUse,Fitzpatrick2025DigitalEquity,Epp2025CoLeadership}.
Community Wellness Centers (CWCs) already offer low-barrier, peer-delivered
support. Their members still need a simple way to find local health resources
and keep track of their own health information without signing up for an app
that tracks them.

Most digital health tools assume accounts, continuous tracking, clinical decision
support, or a chatbot. We ask a different question:
\emph{How much useful, contextually personalized health support can a mobile
application provide while collecting and retaining as little sensitive user data
as possible?} ``Personalized'' here means access shaped by the user's context
(for example a town they name, and health information they keep on the phone).
It does not mean diagnosis or treatment recommendations. We work on this question through
equity-centered co-design with CWC members, peer support specialists, a community
advisory board, and a Rutgers team from behavioral health and electrical and
computer engineering. The prototype, CWC~Health, is a Flutter app with four tabs
(Nearby, My~Health, Learn, More) and a persistent Help~Now button. The current
study build focuses on navigation that is easy to find, source-labeled health
articles, crisis dialers that stay off unless a build enables them, and a live
Nearby tab that geocodes a town name and queries OpenStreetMap instead of using
device GPS. It has no account system and no third-party analytics.

This paper reports Stage~1 co-design methods and findings, the system design of
the current study prototype, and how community themes map to as-built choices.
We relate the work to online health information seeking, mHealth adoption
barriers, participatory digital health, and technical work on privacy-preserving
mobile location practice. We do not claim clinical efficacy. App usability
testing (Stage~3) is future work.

\subsection{Contributions}
The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stage~1 evidence from five CWC focus groups and pre-surveys on health
    information needs, digital hesitancy, privacy concerns, and support
    preferences among members and peer support specialists.
    \item A mobile health navigation design that finds nearby care from a
    town-level geocode and OpenStreetMap queries, with no device GPS tracking
    in the current study build.
    \item A prototype with no user accounts and no third-party analytics.
    My~Health remains sample content in the study build; local-only storage is a
    design target, not a shipped claim.
    \item An explicit mapping from Stage~1 themes to the four-tab study
    interface (Nearby, My~Health, Learn, More) and gated Help~Now dialers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Online Health Information Seeking and Fragmented Access}
People look for health information online, but it is spread across websites,
portals, and social platforms and is hard to
evaluate~\cite{Jia2021OHIS,DiazHernandez2024HealthInfo,Zhao2022OlderOHIS}.
A US national survey (HINTS 2024) found that most social media users engage with
health content there, and most of them also believe much of it is false or
misleading~\cite{Pedroso2026SocialMedia}. Policy commentary ties digital access
programs directly to digital health equity for low-income
populations~\cite{Fitzpatrick2025DigitalEquity}. ``Social prescribing,'' where a
clinician or community worker refers someone to non-medical local supports,
depends on the same thing: a reliable map of what is nearby~\cite{Lancet2025SocialPrescribing}.
This is the gap CWC~Health targets: one place to find nearby resources and
trusted education, without expecting users to assemble a care pathway on their own.

\subsection{Barriers to Mobile and Digital Health Engagement}
A qualitative systematic review of 34 studies found the same barriers to digital
health across culturally and linguistically diverse groups: access, literacy,
language, trust, and usability~\cite{Whitehead2023Barriers}. In an integrated
youth-services network, users and staff said mHealth lowered barriers to
in-person care but raised privacy, design, and sustainability concerns, and they
asked for co-design~\cite{Ding2025mHealth}. Digital literacy is now treated as a
social determinant of health, with people with serious mental illness at the
highest risk of being left out~\cite{Dwyer2025TechUse}. Those barriers motivate CWC~Health's flat four-tab layout, source labels on
every Learn article, and a build with no accounts or analytics.

\subsection{Co-Design and Participatory Digital Health}
Community-based participatory research and co-production hold that community
partners should share decision-making, and that this produces more relevant,
more trusted work~\cite{Opara2025CBPR,PapastavrouBrooks2023Principles}.
Epp et al.\ make the same argument for lived-experience co-leadership in mental
and substance-use care~\cite{Epp2025CoLeadership}. Veldmeijer and van Os warn
that the ``participatory'' label is often absorbed by institutions without any
real transfer of power~\cite{Veldmeijer2025Participatory}. Two recent app
projects show what co-design looks like in practice: adapting App4Independence
for opioid use disorder with 6 lived experts and 8 clinicians, and formative
interviews and focus groups with a community advisory board for a
wellness-enhanced contingency-management app~\cite{Darcey2024A4iO,Deshais2026WECM}.
CWC~Health follows the same approach but for health navigation: nearby care,
personal health organization, education, and urgent support, with as little
stored data as possible and no clinical decision support or generative AI.

\subsection{Privacy-Preserving Mobile Health and Location Practice}
Privacy by design treats data protection as a default system property, not an
afterthought~\cite{Cavoukian2010PbDWorkshop}. Reviews of consumer mHealth apps
still find weak or opaque privacy and data-sharing practices, a poor match for
populations that already distrust digital platforms~\cite{Alfawzan2022mHealthPrivacy}.
Field studies show how often mobile apps share location beyond what users
expect~\cite{Almuhimedi2015LocationShared}. Work on inferring location privacy
preferences without continuous GPS argues that useful place context need not
mean a full tracking
history~\cite{Maekawa2017LocationPrefs}. Crowdsourced map layers such as
OpenStreetMap and commercial amenity databases also diverge in coverage, so a
navigation app that relies on either source must treat listings as imperfect and
say so~\cite{Haklay2010OSM,Mara2025OSMGoogleAmenities}. National crisis lines such as 988 are
evaluated as call outcomes infrastructure; putting those numbers one tap away is
a navigation choice, not a substitute for clinical crisis
care~\cite{Gould2025Lifeline988}. CWC~Health takes the engineering side of these
points: no accounts, no analytics SDKs, town-level geocode instead of device GPS,
OSM-backed Nearby with an unvetted-listing disclaimer, and Help~Now dialers that
stay off unless a build explicitly enables them.

\subsection{Design Goals}
CWC Health is a health-navigation app, not a clinical decision-support system.
It helps users find care, keep their own health information on the phone,
read source-labeled health articles, and reach urgent help quickly. It does
not diagnose or recommend treatment. Three goals drive the design: (i)~collect as little data as possible (no
accounts, no third-party analytics), (ii)~a layout CWC members can navigate in
co-design sessions, and (iii)~live resource discovery that does not depend on
tracking the user's location. The Flutter study prototype implements the navigation shell and the
live Nearby tab. Several My~Health and offline-directory behaviors are still
design targets in the project specification; each is marked as such below.

\subsection{Application Structure}
The study build is a Flutter cross-platform client~\cite{FlutterDocs2026} with
four bottom tabs (Nearby, My~Health, Learn, More) and a Help~Now button in the
header, so urgent actions are one tap away from any screen (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}
and Fig.~\ref{fig:ui_mockups}). Nearby is the default tab. Two compile-time flags
gate network behavior. \texttt{LIVE\_NEARBY} (default off) swaps the static Nearby
demo list for live OpenStreetMap results. \texttt{HELP\_NOW\_LIVE} (default off)
turns the 988, 911, and Poison Control buttons into real \texttt{tel:} and
\texttt{sms:} launches~\cite{Gould2025Lifeline988}. The co-design mockups in
Fig.~\ref{fig:ui_mockups} show the intended information architecture. In the
current study build, My~Health and Learn still show sample content for co-design
feedback, and More opens placeholder settings and support pages. Its Erase
button currently explains that nothing is stored on the phone yet.

\subsection{Privacy and Data Minimization}
The prototype follows a data-minimization approach aligned with privacy by
design~\cite{Cavoukian2010PbDWorkshop,Alfawzan2022mHealthPrivacy}. It has no user
accounts, stores no login credentials, and includes no third-party analytics SDKs
(its only runtime dependencies are \texttt{http}, \texttt{url\_launcher}, and
\texttt{shared\_preferences}). Live Nearby keeps two things on the device: the
last successful listing and the town geocode point. There is no identity and no
GPS history~\cite{Almuhimedi2015LocationShared,Maekawa2017LocationPrefs}. Help~Now dialers stay off
unless a build sets \texttt{HELP\_NOW\_LIVE}, so a demo cannot place a real
crisis call by accident. The feature specification also calls for an optional
PIN on My~Health, encrypted on-device records, and a working one-tap erase.
None of those are in the current study build, and we do not claim them here.

\subsection{Location-Aware Nearby Services}
With \texttt{LIVE\_NEARBY} on, Nearby geocodes a fixed town name (New Brunswick,
NJ, in the v1 engineering demo) through Nominatim~\cite{NominatimDocs} and queries
OpenStreetMap Overpass for pharmacies, clinics, and urgent
care~\cite{OverpassDocs,Haklay2010OSM,Mara2025OSMGoogleAmenities}. Fig.~\ref{fig:nearby_pipeline}
summarizes the path. Results are filtered to a New~Jersey bounding-box guard so
obvious out-of-state points are dropped. The client tries three Overpass mirrors
in order, because Overpass rate-limits per IP and a room of phones on one Wi-Fi
network shares an IP. An optional Google Places lookup falls through to
OpenStreetMap if no billed key is set. The live path never requests location
permission and uses no background location APIs. Listings are deduplicated,
time-stamped, and written to an on-device last-success cache. When OSM
\texttt{opening\_hours} tags parse, cards show Open now / Closed with an
expandable weekday view; missing or unparseable tags show ``Hours not listed.''
If every source fails, the tab shows an error and a retry button. It never falls
back to the demo list, because a user cannot tell a fake pharmacy from a real
one. Directions open OpenStreetMap on web or a native \texttt{geo:} link on
device, with no embedded map SDK. Builds with the flag off keep the static
New~Brunswick demo list for meetings and tests.

\subsection{On-Device Personal Health Information}
My~Health shows appointments, medications, providers, and a wallet card. In the
current build these are sample constants, not saved user data, and the screen
says My~Health is not PIN-locked yet. Learn has six topics with short articles
and a named source on each (for example, public-health agencies). It does not
generate AI answers. The longer-term target is a local-only personal-health
store with an optional PIN and a working erase.

\subsection{Study Design and Aims}
Stage~1 is a needs assessment for a smartphone health-navigation app used in
Community Wellness Centers operated with Collaborative Support Programs of New
Jersey (CSPNJ). The aim was to learn how CWC members and peer support specialists
seek health information, what local resources they use, what makes a health app
usable or untrustworthy, and what support they want when using such a tool. A
community advisory board with lived experience helped shape recruitment language
and interpretation. Later project stages cover app design, alpha work, and usability testing.
This paper reports Stage~1 and the as-built study prototype that followed.

\subsection{Setting and Participants}
Recruitment targeted adults (18+) who could consent, engage with app-related
discussion, and speak English, drawn from CSPNJ CWCs across New Jersey regions.
Peer support specialists were recruited for virtual sessions so staff at multiple
sites could join without travel. Members were recruited for in-person sessions at
the centers where they already receive services. Five focus groups were
completed: two virtual peer-support groups (February 2026) and three in-person
member groups (March 2026) at CWCs in Glassboro, Jersey City, and Neptune.
Compensated attendance recorded in study gift-card clearing records was $n{=}7$
peer support specialists (virtual) and $n{=}38$ CWC members (in person). Each
participant received a \$50 gift card.

\subsection{Procedures}
Virtual sessions used Zoom with a waiting room, informed consent before
discussion, and host-only audio recording. In-person sessions used written
consent read aloud, audio recording, and a short Qualtrics pre-survey on laptop
or phone before the discussion. Facilitator scripts covered health information
access, local resources, technology and health apps, trust/distrust, and support
preferences. Sessions were planned for about 75--90 minutes. Recordings were
transcribed and de-identified before analysis.

\subsection{Pre-Survey}
Members and peer support specialists completed Qualtrics pre-surveys on
demographics, smartphone access and comfort, health information seeking, and
preferred help for using a health app. Item-level $n$ varies because skip logic
routed respondents without smartphone access away from later phone items. In the
summary used here, member demographics are reported for $n{=}41$, and
smartphone-specific member items for about $n{=}27$. Peer-support items are
reported for about $n{=}9$--10 depending on the question.

\subsection{Analysis}
Focus-group transcripts were analyzed with iterative qualitative content
analysis. Reviewers familiarized themselves with each transcript and wrote
memos. A codebook combined deductive codes from the study aims/guide with
inductive codes from participant language. Two reviewers independently coded
meaning units, met to resolve disagreements, and used a third reviewer as
adjudicator when needed. Codes were grouped into higher-order categories for
member and peer-support summaries. Trustworthiness relied on multiple coders,
consensus meetings, memoing, and an audit trail of coding decisions.

\subsection{Ethics}
The Rutgers Human Research Protection Program approved the study as Exempt~3b
(IRB number Pro2025002116; approval date 19~November~2025). The determination
was minimal risk. Consent covered recording, confidentiality, and the right to
withdraw.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Pre-Survey Highlights}
Among members with demographic responses ($n{=}41$), 46\% were unemployed and
12\% were unemployed and receiving SSI/SSDI; 48\% reported some form of unhoused
status (24\% shelter, 24\% unsheltered). Highest completed education was often high school or less (44\% high school
or equivalent; 17\% some high school without a diploma). Just over half
(54\%) were age 45 or older. Smartphone access was incomplete: 63\% of members
reported a smartphone ($n{=}41$ base for that item), and most without a phone
also lacked regular access to someone else's device.

Among members with smartphone access ($n{=}27$), use was frequent (81\% multiple
times a day), mostly Android (78\%), with mean comfort $3.41$ on a 1--4 scale.
Health-app use was low: 63\% used health apps ``rarely'' or ``never.'' Most of
these respondents sought health information (89\%), often daily (46\%), mainly
via internet search (67\%), then providers (38\%) and friends/family (33\%).
Mean confidence finding health information was $3.26$ / 4. A large majority
(89\%) said a smartphone health app would be helpful, and 67\% wanted help using
one (Fig.~\ref{fig:presurvey}). Preferred help formats included video walkthroughs (42\%) and peer support
specialists (33\%). Members most often learned about wellness resources by word
of mouth (59\%) and family/friends (48\%).

Peer-support pre-survey items ($n{\approx}9$--10) showed high smartphone comfort
(mean $4$ / 4 where reported) and similar interest in a helpful health app, with
staff often positioned as the people who already help members navigate
technology.

\subsection{Member Focus-Group Themes}
Member discussions emphasized physical health routines (appointments,
medications, exercise, diet), mental health and recovery practices, and social
connection through CWCs and peers. Holistic, multi-domain wellness descriptions
were common. Information seeking mixed internet and AI tools with providers and
caseworkers, but experiential and peer knowledge were highly valued.
Action-oriented needs stood out: participants wanted next steps, clinics to
call, and practical guidance rather than abstract content alone. Resource access
centered on trusted local people and places, including CWCs. Barriers included
transportation, fragmented services, and low awareness of programs.

Digital tool hesitancy was frequent. Some members preferred phone appointments
over apps, disliked apps, or relied on a relative or caseworker to operate
technology. Plain language and walkthrough support were recurring requests.
Privacy and trust concerns covered scams, data misuse, and security; trust was
often placed in specific community staff or peers rather than generic platforms.

\subsection{Peer Support Specialist Themes}
Peer-support discussions (28 coded excerpts in the staff summary table) stressed
health information seeking, resource access, and ambivalence about digital tools:
convenience versus misinformation, and difficulty sustaining app use. Staff
wanted simple interfaces, reminders, credentialed content, and support for
self-tracking of medications and appointments. Technology support needs were
prominent from the staff vantage point (staff-assisted navigation and
institutional support). Structural barriers for members, including phone loss and
unstable housing, were described explicitly.

\subsection{Design Implications for the Study Prototype}
Stage~1 themes map onto the as-built study build as summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:theme_map} and Fig.~\ref{fig:theme_map}. Centralized local
resource discovery and action-oriented ``what is nearby / who to call'' needs
drove the Nearby tab and live OSM listings, with an unvetted-data disclaimer
because map sources are imperfect~\cite{Haklay2010OSM,Mara2025OSMGoogleAmenities}. Distrust of
data-heavy apps and login friction drove the no-account, no-analytics approach and town-level geocode instead of
device GPS. Plain-language, source-labeled
education drove Learn. Appointment and medication self-management needs drove the
My~Health information architecture, still populated with sample data until
local-only persistence ships. Peer-supported onboarding and crisis access drove
the persistent Help~Now entry point, with live 988/911 dialers gated so demos
cannot place accidental calls~\cite{Gould2025Lifeline988}. Simplicity and one
job per tab drove the four-tab shell. These are design responses to Stage~1
evidence, not yet Stage~3 usability measurements of the Flutter build.

\section{Discussion}
Stage~1 shows a clear gap: many CWC members already use smartphones often, but
health apps are rare, trust is fragile, and help is expected from peers and
staff. ``Personalized'' support in this project means contextual access (a town
search, local listings, on-device health organization), not diagnosis or treatment
recommendation. Privacy-preserving engineering is a design requirement here, not a slogan.
Participants named scams, surveillance-like collection, and login burden as
reasons to avoid apps, which matches broader mHealth privacy
findings~\cite{Alfawzan2022mHealthPrivacy,Almuhimedi2015LocationShared}.

From an engineering standpoint, the study build trades continuous sensing for
explicit, inspectable network paths. Town geocoding plus OSM queries give place
context without a GPS permission prompt~\cite{Maekawa2017LocationPrefs,NominatimDocs}.
Mirror failover and an on-device last-success cache are practical responses to
shared-IP rate limits and intermittent connectivity in community-center demos.
Compile-time flags (\texttt{LIVE\_NEARBY}, \texttt{HELP\_NOW\_LIVE}) separate
meeting-safe builds from live field builds without forking the UI.

The study prototype is intentionally narrow. It navigates to care and education.
It does not replace peer support, clinical portals, or crisis counselors. Putting 988 one tap away is a navigation affordance, not a clinical crisis
service, and it sits alongside evidence that crisis lines matter as
infrastructure~\cite{Gould2025Lifeline988}. Co-design with members
and peer specialists, plus a community advisory board, is how the team tries to avoid ``participatory'' labeling without shared
decision-making~\cite{Veldmeijer2025Participatory,Epp2025CoLeadership}.

\section{Limitations}
Results are Stage~1 only. We do not report task-completion rates, Help~Now
discoverability metrics, or other Stage~3 usability measures for the Flutter
build. Focus-group and pre-survey samples are tied to participating CSPNJ sites
and English-speaking inclusion criteria; they are not a statewide probability
sample. Gift-card clearing aggregates document compensated attendance but are not
a full demographic census of every person who spoke in a room. OSM coverage and
tag quality vary by amenity and region~\cite{Haklay2010OSM,Mara2025OSMGoogleAmenities},
and listings are not independently verified by the study team. Phone numbers are
often missing on OSM rows, so Call / Text may be hidden while Directions remain.
My~Health is still demo content. Encrypted on-device storage, PIN lock, and a
working erase remain spec targets. We claim no clinical outcomes.

\section{Conclusion}
CWC members and peer support specialists asked for simple, trustworthy ways to
find local care, keep track of personal health tasks, read plain-language
education, and reach urgent help without giving an app a large data trail.
Stage~1 focus groups and pre-surveys document those needs with concrete $n$s and
themes. The current CWC~Health study build answers with a four-tab Flutter shell,
town-geocoded OSM Nearby (mirror failover, hours parse, on-device last-success
cache), gated Help~Now dialers, and no accounts or third-party analytics. Next
work is Stage~3 usability testing of that as-built prototype with the same
communities, plus finishing local-only My~Health persistence.
